Sources and method

Twenty-four public records, one point, a four-step score per section, and a checks line that says exactly what was checked

Every figure in a Groundsheet names its source and the day it was retrieved. This page is the ledger behind that habit. It lists what each source answers, at what resolution, from what vintage and under what licence; explains the five-step scale and the composite; says what "Checks: passed" means and does not mean; states the screening limits; and shows how to cite a figure.

What happens when a report is run

The address is geocoded by the US Census Geocoder to a point; the match quality is printed on the cover (for the Aptos sample, "Census exact match", with the TIGER line it matched). Around that point the 24 public datasets below are queried, each at the screening distance its section states: two miles for regulated facilities and wells, five miles for airports, five kilometres for pipelines, 50 km for burn perimeters, about 20 miles for critical habitat, half a mile for wetlands, one mile for schools and clinics. Each source's answer is kept with the time it answered. The sections are scored, the composite is computed, every figure the prose prints is reconciled back to its record, the codes the pages print are checked against the key, and the PDF is rendered. The whole run takes minutes.

The scale and the composite

Each of the sixteen sections is scored on a four-step scale, 0 to 3, from its federal source record, and shown on a 0 to 10 display scale as 0, 3.3, 6.7 or 10. Each section has a fixed weight; the weights sum to 100 percent and the weighted sum is the composite, 2.9 of 10 in the Aptos sample. A section marked not assessed carries no score and no weight. The composite falls into one of four bands, and the word on the cover is the band:

Lowcomposite below 2.1: nothing within screening distance carries a hazardous designation; standard due diligence
Moderate2.1 to 4.0: some factors warrant attention; enhanced due diligence before acquisition
Elevated4.1 to 6.5: multiple risk factors, or Tier I or II facility weight near the site; a Phase I ESA strongly recommended
High6.6 and above: significant concerns across categories; a Phase I ESA required
Severenot a band but a trigger: a Superfund (NPL) site within 0.5 mi overrides the composite; expect Phase II sampling

The word beside a section is its own band, not the composite's: a section at High inside a Moderate composite is a real High, and the findings ledger on pages 4 and 5 of every report lists the sections at High or Elevated first so none can hide behind the average.

What "Checks: passed" means

Every cover carries a checks line with one of three words. It is printed by the run that made the report, from its own record of what happened:

  • Checks: passed. Every core source answered (the geocoder, EPA facilities, FEMA flood, USFS wildfire, the community context query), every figure the text prints was reconciled to the record it came from, and every cross-check passed. This is a retrieval and consistency check, not a verification of the underlying public records.
  • Degraded. Complete and honest, but a source was not available or a check could not run; the gaps are named in the sources ledger at the back of the report and the section a missing source feeds is marked not assessed.
  • Failed. A check found a contradiction; do not rely on the report.

The samples rendered on 13 October 2026 still print the older label, "Integrity: verified", with the same meaning; the label changes to "Checks: passed" in the next render so the cover cannot be read as a warranty of the records. Numeric reconciliation means every number in the prose was matched against its source record before the report was issued; a mismatch sets the status to Failed. What the checks do not do: they do not confirm that EPA's, FEMA's or the state's record is itself correct, current or complete. A facility missing from ECHO is missing from the report; a flood map revision in progress is not in the effective panel; a school absent from OpenStreetMap is absent here.

The screening limits

A Groundsheet is a screening of public records at one point. It is not a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment under ASTM E1527-21 and does not satisfy All Appropriate Inquiries under 40 CFR Part 312; it carries no CERCLA liability protection. It is not a home inspection, not a statutory disclosure (in California, not a Natural Hazard Disclosure Statement), not an insurance opinion and not an engineering opinion. It does not visit the site, interview anyone, review historical records or search state and local databases beyond the sources named above. Distances are measured from the geocoded point, not from parcel lines, until a parcel source is wired in; the cover says so. Scores are relative screening indicators generated by the fixed rules above; they can miss recent events, state-level actions and anything not in the federal record. Where a finding matters, the report names the specialist: an environmental professional for a Phase I, a surveyor for an elevation certificate, a geotechnical engineer, a biologist, a wetland delineator, a certified radon mitigator, a licensed geologist for a sinkhole inspection.

How to cite a figure

Cite the source and the date in the figure's collar, then the report. The collar on every map carries source, vintage, retrieved date and projection, so the citation is on the page:

FEMA National Flood Hazard Layer, zone record retrieved 13 Oct 2026, FIRM panel 06087C; plate from Groundsheet report PLM-20261013174435, 123 Post Office Dr, Aptos, CA, 13 October 2026, page 12.

For a figure on this site, add the page URL and the date in its "Updated" line. For the sample reports themselves: Aptos, California and Tampa, Florida. For every code: the codes key. For what each section answers: what's inside.
