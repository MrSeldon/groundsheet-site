§ 06 · Wetlands

Every mapped wetland within half a mile, decoded, and the permits a project near one meets

The National Wetlands Inventory maps wetlands from imagery at a planning scale, which makes it a reason to look and not a boundary. The section draws every feature within half a mile, decodes each Cowardin code into words, measures the nearest edge, and sets out the permits in the order a project meets them. Aptos has four features, the nearest a forested wetland 411 ft away; none contains the point.

What the figures show

The map draws every NWI polygon within half a mile over satellite imagery, filled by its Cowardin system (the first letter of the code) and labelled with the full code, with the subject ring at the centre and the nearest edge measured. The five systems are keyed on the page: palustrine (freshwater marsh, swamp, bog, small pond), lacustrine (lakes and ponds over 20 acres), riverine (water within a channel), estuarine (tidal, salt to brackish) and marine (open ocean and its coastline). The second page is a ledger of the features nearest first, each code decoded into its type, water regime and modifiers, with acres and distance; then the permits block, federal, state and local, in the order a project meets them; then the steps and the annex.

At Aptos the four features tell a small story about a creek: PFOA, a temporarily flooded forested wetland of 19.4 acres, 411 ft away; PFOR, a sliver of forested wetland that is seasonally flooded and fresh-tidal, 793 ft; R4SBC, an intermittent streambed, 1,802 ft; and R1UBV, the tidal, permanently flooded reach of the stream, 2,326 ft. The creek runs to a lagoon, and the tidal letters say so.

What it cannot show

NWI polygons are drawn from aerial imagery at a planning scale, so edges can be off by tens of feet and small or forested wetlands are often missed entirely; the map is a reason to look, not a boundary. It is not a jurisdictional determination: whether a wetland is a "water of the United States" is the Corps' decision, and since Sackett (2023) only wetlands with a continuous surface connection to a permanent water are federal. Nor is it a delineation, the field survey that stakes the actual edge. A polygon 411 ft away means the parcel is not inside a mapped wetland; it does not mean runoff from the lot cannot reach one, or that a local buffer rule does not apply.

Why it matters to a buyer

Wetlands change what can be built and where. Placing fill in a jurisdictional wetland or stream needs a Section 404 permit from the Army Corps; grading, roads, culverts and drainage are the usual triggers. Small residential fills under half an acre can use a nationwide permit with a notification; larger work needs an individual permit with public notice. The state must certify the federal permit under Section 401, and in California the Regional Water Quality Control Board also regulates fill in wetlands the federal rule no longer reaches. In the coastal zone the local coastal programme adds sensitive-habitat buffers, often 100 ft; counties and cities set their own setbacks of 50 to 100 ft from wetlands and streams, with their own variance paths. Those buffers apply whether or not the wetland is federal, which is why a mapped feature a few hundred feet away belongs on a site plan before it is drawn. The Groundsheet scale weights wetlands at 3 percent; Aptos reads Elevated on proximity.

What you can do

  1. Look at the mapped polygons yourself on the national wetlands mapper; the edge drawn nearest the Aptos lot is 411 ft away, and walk that ground for standing water and wet-soil plants. fws.gov
  2. If a project reaches the mapped edge or wet ground, get a delineation and the Corps' jurisdictional determination before grading or filling. usace.army.mil
  3. The regional water board issues the matching state certification, and in the coastal zone the local plan adds its own buffer; ask before drawing a site plan. waterboards.ca.gov
  4. Check the county's own wetland and stream setback in its code; it applies whether or not the wetland is federal.
