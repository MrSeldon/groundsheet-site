§ 04 · Geology, wells and seismic

What is under the house, every well on record around it, and how hard the ground is expected to shake

Three records on two pages. Every well completion report within two miles drawn from above, one glyph per well, so the water under the neighbourhood reads at a glance; the mapped rock unit under the lot with its age; and the building code's seismic design category, which describes the ground and not the building. Aptos has 50 wells in 2 mi, Pleistocene alluvium underfoot and Category D, high shaking.

What the figures show

The hero is the wells map, drawn the way a hydrogeologist would sketch it: every well on record within two miles seen from above, one glyph per well. The circle's area is the yield in gallons per minute (hollow when none was recorded), the needle hanging south is the drilled depth at the map's own scale, and the thick part of the needle is the screen, where water enters. Rings every half mile, faded arterial roads and water for orientation, never a full basemap. Where several wells share one snapped point they fan out and the count is printed. A small key beside it shows one glyph decoded and a bar chart of every well with a depth, nearest to farthest, with the median drawn. The second page shows the geologic map (the surface units coloured and lettered, fault traces drawn) and a strata panel that places the unit under the lot in time on a square-root scale, with its lithology and what it means for a foundation, for karst, for a spill and for excavation; then the seismic design values on the A-to-E scale with the site's letter marked.

At Aptos the map says something the address alone would not: the village is on city water, yet 50 completion reports lie within two miles and 29 within a quarter mile, a monitoring well 0.16 mi south-west (the kind that watches water near a tank or cleanup site), a public-supply well 0.17 mi north-north-east at 743 ft and 250 gpm, and an industrial well at 780 gpm. The ground is alluvial fan deposits of the Pleistocene, 0.01 to 2.58 million years old: loose sand, gravel and mud dropped by streams leaving the hills, which drains well, holds shallow groundwater, and amplifies shaking. The design category is D.

What it cannot show

A completion report records the well as drilled, not as it is today: 18 of the 50 are of unknown use, 18 lack a depth and 37 lack a yield. The database is national and lags the state's own well log archive, so a well drilled last year may be missing. The geologic unit is the mapped surface unit at the state map's scale; a true multi-unit column under the lot needs a boring log. The seismic values are mapped hazard on an assumed firm site (class BC, VS30 760 m/s); soft alluvium like Aptos's amplifies shaking, so the real motion at the footing can be worse than the mapped number. And above all: the design category describes the ground, not the building. What this house does in the next earthquake depends on whether it is bolted, braced and strapped, which only an inspector can see.

Why it matters to a buyer

On a private well, the neighbours' completion reports are the best free guide to what a new well will find: depth to water, yield, and the aquifer. On city water, the nearest public-supply well is a receptor: anything moving through the ground from a regulated facility shows there first, which is why the facilities section and this one are read together. The seismic category sets what the building code requires for new work (in D: ductile detailing, anchored equipment, engineered foundations) and what a retrofit of an older house should address; it also frames the earthquake insurance decision, which in California is a separate policy. Geology and soil carry 7 percent of the Groundsheet composite and seismic hazard 8 percent; Aptos scores High on seismic, which is why the cover's recommendation names a seismic retrofit inspection first.

What you can do

  1. On a private well, ask for the completion report, the static water level and a water test from the past year. data.cnra.ca.gov
  2. Test a private well yearly for bacteria and nitrate, and once for what the facilities in § 01 handle. epa.gov
  3. On city water, read the system's annual water quality report: its source wells and every contaminant found. epa.gov
  4. Ask the inspector: is the house bolted to its foundation, any cripple wall braced, the water heater strapped? crmp.org
  5. Fit an automatic gas shut-off valve at the meter, or at least know where the manual valve and the wrench are. usgs.gov
  6. Keep the federal kit (three days of water, food, medicines, a radio) and turn on the state's early-warning phone alerts. ready.gov
  7. Earthquake insurance is separate from a homeowner policy; the California Earthquake Authority writes most of it. earthquakeauthority.com

Reading this section

USGWD
United States Groundwater Well Database, 14.2 million well records compiled from state completion reports, published on HydroShare in 2023.
gpm
Gallons per minute, the yield recorded at completion; drawn as the circle's area.
Screen
The slotted length of casing where water enters the well; the thick part of the needle.
Monitoring well
A well drilled to watch water quality or level, typically near a tank, a landfill or a cleanup site, not to supply water.
Public supply
A well feeding a drinking-water system regulated under the Safe Drinking Water Act; a receptor.
SDC
Seismic Design Category, A to E, from the design values and the risk category of the building; D means strong shaking expected in a building's life.
SDS · SD1
Design shaking at 0.2 s (stiff low buildings) and 1 s (tall flexible ones) as a share of gravity; SS and S1 are the mapped values before the site adjustment.
PGA
Peak ground acceleration at the 2 percent in 50 years motion, as a share of gravity.
Site class BC · VS30
The assumed firm-ground class and its shear-wave velocity, 760 m/s; soft ground amplifies the mapped values.
ASCE 7-22
The load standard the building code adopts; values for Risk Category II, ordinary buildings.
Ma
Million years ago; Pleistocene, the ice ages, 2.58 million to 11,700 years ago.
Macrostrat
The geologic database that names the unit under the subject from the state map (CGS 2010 in California).
CGS
California Geological Survey, author of the geologic map and the fault and seismic hazard zones.
Alluvium, alluvial fan
Loose sand, gravel and mud dropped by streams leaving the hills; soft ground that amplifies shaking and settles unevenly.
