§ 07 · Pipelines

Transmission lines within five kilometres, the operator, and how close the nearest centreline runs

The public pipeline record is deliberately coarse, and the section says so in its collar. The Energy Information Administration's layers give generalised centrelines for natural gas transmission and hazardous liquid lines; the section counts them within one, two and five kilometres, names the operator and measures the nearest. Aptos has seven gas segments, one operator, the nearest 0.8 km to the west, and no hazardous liquid line.

What the figure shows

A light basemap with every transmission segment within five kilometres drawn as the public layer gives it, gas in one weight and hazardous liquid in another, the subject at the centre, and a bar chart of counts within one, two and five kilometres. A line of text names each operator with its segment count and whether the line crosses a state border. The collar states the one thing to know about the geometry: these are the Energy Information Administration's generalised centrelines, drawn for national maps, not survey lines; the precise alignment lives in PHMSA's National Pipeline Mapping System, which the public can view one county at a time under access rules. Aptos: seven segments of one operator's gas transmission, three within a kilometre, the nearest about 0.83 km to the west; no hazardous liquid.

What it cannot show

Where the line is to the metre. Whether an easement crosses the parcel (that is the title report's job). Distribution lines, the smaller pipes under every street, which are not in the transmission layer at all. The pipe's age, pressure, inspection history or incident record, which PHMSA holds by operator, not by map. And setbacks: there is no federal setback from a transmission line; what exists is local, in zoning or subdivision rules, and varies by county.

Why it matters to a buyer

Three practical things. The law requires a call to 811 two working days before any digging, even for a fence post, and the marking is free; the operator marks its line on the ground. A transmission easement on the parcel limits what can be built over it and gives the operator access. And a line nearby is something an insurer or a lender may ask about, which is easier answered with the map than without it. The Groundsheet scale weights pipelines at 4 percent from distance; Aptos reads Moderate at 0.8 km.

What you can do

  1. Call 811 two working days before any digging, even a fence post; the law requires it and the marking is free. phmsa.dot.gov
  2. Confirm the alignment on the national pipeline map viewer and ask the title company for any easement on the parcel. pvnpms.phmsa.dot.gov
  3. Know the leak signs (rotten-egg smell, hissing, a strip of dead plants) and the operator's number on the line markers. phmsa.dot.gov
  4. Ask the county planning desk whether a pipeline setback applies to any addition you have in mind; it is local, not federal.
