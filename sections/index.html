What's inside the report

Eleven sections and two inserts, each answering one question about the ground under a house

A Groundsheet runs section by section, each from its own public record, each with a map that says where it came from and when. These pages unfold every section from the two sample reports, Aptos in California and Tampa in Florida, and explain what the figure shows, what it cannot show, what a buyer does with it, and every code it prints.

§ 01

Regulated facilities

Which regulated facilities sit within two miles, and which of them matter: 708 in Tampa, 263 in Aptos, and in both cases the tier, not the count, is the finding.

How the sections run

A report opens with the cover, a two-page executive summary and a two-page findings ledger that lists every section with its level and one line of fact, the Highs first. Then the sections, in a fixed order: § 01 regulated facilities, § 02 flood and terrain, § 03 wildfire, § 04 geology, wells and seismic, § 05 listed species, § 06 wetlands, § 07 pipelines, § 08 community context, § 09 air quality. Three parts are conditional and unnumbered or state-specific: the California hazard zones insert (California only, before § 01), the radon insert (where the county is EPA Zone 1 or 2), the karst insert inside § 04 (where carbonate rock is mapped, so far Florida), and § 10 wind and storms (where a tornado of EF2 or more has passed within 10 miles or a hurricane within 50). A section with nothing to report says so in one page rather than disappearing: an empty fire roster is a finding, not missing data.

Every section follows one contract: a headline that states the finding, not the topic; one hero figure with a collar naming its source, vintage, retrieval date and projection; four stats; a body in plain words; a "What you can do" block with official links; and a "Reading this section" annex that defines every code the page printed. The back of the report repeats the whole vocabulary in the codes key, and the method pages explain the sources and the scale.

Both samples are free to read: Aptos, California (40 pages) and Tampa, Florida (40 pages).
