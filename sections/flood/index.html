What the figure shows

The first figure is the flood map most people have seen, drawn the way it should be: FEMA's zone polygons over the real terrain, tilted so the valley reads as a valley, each polygon tinted on a five-class blue palette (darkest for the coastal VE zones, lightest for X) and lettered with its zone so the colour never carries a fact alone. The subject is marked with its elevation in feet; the nearest special flood hazard area is named, measured and given a bearing. A second figure, the parcel-detail inset, appears only when a mapped zone comes close: a top-down view at parcel zoom with the same palette, so you can see exactly how the Zone AE edge along the creek relates to the lot. Aptos triggers it at 0.09 mi; a lot a mile from any zone would not.

The section reads the zone from one dated NFHL point record, the same record the scorer and the summary use, so the page, the score and the cover cannot disagree. A separate live query draws the outlines for the plate; if that fails, the zone stands and the page says so; if no record returns, no zone is printed and the section is marked not assessed rather than guessed.

What it cannot show

The polygons are FEMA's mapped polygons at regional scale, not survey grade: a boundary 0.09 mi from a lot is close enough to confirm, not to read to the foot off this map. The map is the effective panel, so a revision in progress (a letter of map revision, a new study) is not in it. Zone X is "minimal hazard", not "no hazard": about a quarter of flood claims come from outside the mapped floodplain, from drainage, groundwater and storm surge the panel does not model. The subject elevation is the ground at the geocoded point, not the finished floor, and the base flood elevation that sets freeboard is on the FIRM panel, not on this page. And the whole section is about riverine and coastal flooding as FEMA maps it; a crawlspace that floods from a high water table is a seller question, not a map question.

Why it matters to a buyer

Inside a special flood hazard area (Zones A, AE, AH, AO, V, VE), flood insurance is mandatory on a federally backed mortgage, and the premium follows the zone and an elevation certificate; outside it, insurance is optional and cheap, and most people skip it until the one year they should not have. The zone also drives the California hazard zones disclosure (row 1 of the six) and the building rules for any addition. The Groundsheet scale weights this section at 12 percent, the third-largest weight, scored from the zone class alone.

What you can do

  1. Zone X still floods: about a quarter of flood claims come from outside the mapped floodplain, and the low-risk policy costs least, so get a quote before closing. floodsmart.gov
  2. Check the effective flood map panel and any revision in progress at the Flood Map Service Center; for Aptos the nearest mapped zone is 0.09 mi south-west. msc.fema.gov
  3. Ask the seller in writing about water in the crawlspace, basement or garage and about any past claim on the building; a paid claim stays with the address in the federal record. fema.gov
  4. Keep a flood plan: the route to higher ground, where to shut off power, and a go-kit by the door. ready.gov
  5. Near an edge, order an elevation certificate from a surveyor; it is the document an insurer prices from and the one a map appeal rests on.

Reading this section

Zone VE / V
The highest-risk coastal areas, exposed to wave action during a major flood; mandatory flood insurance.
Zone A / AE
The 100-year floodplain, a 1 percent chance of flooding in any year; the core special flood hazard area; AE carries a base flood elevation.
Zone AH / AO
Shallow flooding, typically 1 to 3 ft of ponding or sheet flow during the base flood.
Zone X
Shaded: the 500-year floodplain, a 0.2 percent annual chance, or land behind a levee; insurance recommended. Unshaded: minimal hazard, outside the mapped floodplain.
Zone D
Flood hazard undetermined; no analysis has been completed for the area.
SFHA
Special Flood Hazard Area: the land FEMA expects the 1-percent-annual-chance flood to reach; federally backed mortgages here require flood insurance.
100-year, 500-year
Annual probability, not frequency: a 100-year flood has a 1 percent chance in any year and can occur in consecutive years; a 500-year flood, 0.2 percent.
BFE
Base flood elevation: the height floodwater is expected to reach in the 1-percent flood, in feet NAVD 88; subject elevation against BFE reads freeboard.
NAVD 88
North American Vertical Datum of 1988, the reference surface for elevations, so a parcel can be compared with a BFE.
FEMA NFHL
National Flood Hazard Layer, FEMA's digital flood-map database; the legally in-force maps are the effective FIRM panels, and the record date stamps which panel was read.
FIRM
Flood Insurance Rate Map, the panel in force for the area; 06087C for Santa Cruz County.
USGS 3DEP
3D Elevation Program, the source of the relief and the subject elevation.
Behind levee
Land mapped at reduced risk while a levee is certified; residual risk remains if it is overtopped or fails.
Open water
Rivers, lakes and bays mapped by the NFHL but not given a flood class; hatched so the imagery beneath shows the water.
Parcel-detail inset
The close-up top-down map drawn when a mapped zone falls near the subject; omitted when the nearest zone is far away.
EPSG:3857 · WGS 84
Web Mercator on the WGS 84 datum.
