§ 05 · Listed species

Which listed species the county carries, which have critical habitat near the site, and what the law does on private land

Two counts that are often confused, kept apart on purpose. The county list is every species protected under the Endangered Species Act whose range touches the county, 35 for Santa Cruz County; critical habitat is the narrower set of ground mapped by rule as essential to recovery, and 11 of the 35 have some within about twenty miles of the Aptos sample, the nearest 0.3 mi away.

What the figures show

The map draws every critical habitat polygon within about twenty miles of the property, filled by the species' status (endangered or threatened), named on the plate and numbered to a ledger that gives, for each of the species with habitat near the site, the distance and bearing from the property point to the nearest polygon edge, the date of the Federal Register rule that mapped the habitat, the ECOS code and the rule citation. Below the ledger, a block on what the law does on private land. Then a card for each of those species, nearest first, with a plate drawn from field-guide descriptions, why it was listed, its habitat, and one line for a buyer; and finally the full county roster by taxon, with the card numbers beside the names that have habitat nearby, so the two counts can be read against each other.

Aptos carries a long county list because Santa Cruz County runs from the sea to the sandhills: 35 species from the short-tailed albatross and the southern sea otter to the Zayante band-winged grasshopper, an insect known from nowhere else. Eleven have critical habitat within reach of the sample point: the tidewater goby, a lagoon fish, 0.3 mi south-west; the robust spineflower 1.4 mi north-east; the Monterey spineflower 2.2 mi east; the Santa Cruz tarplant 2.4 mi north-west; the California red-legged frog 3.6 mi south-east; and six more out to the California tiger salamander at 14.1 mi. Tampa, by contrast, returned no listed species from the county query, and the report omits the section and says so in the findings ledger.

What it cannot show

A county list says a species' range touches the county, offshore seabirds to sandhill plants, whether or not any occurs on this street; it is where a biologist starts, not a finding about the parcel. Many listed species have no critical habitat designated at all, so absence from the map is not absence from the ground. A distance of 0.3 mi means mapped habitat a short way off, not habitat on the lot. The map cannot see whether a listed animal actually uses the parcel; only a site visit can. And the designation binds no private owner by itself: what it does depends on the project and on whether a federal agency is involved.

Why it matters to a buyer

Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act is the consultation duty: a federal agency must check with USFWS before it permits, funds or approves work that could harm a listed species or its critical habitat. It reaches private land only through that federal nexus, most often an Army Corps wetland fill permit, a federally backed loan for construction, or a grant. Section 9 is the prohibition on take, and it applies on private land with no federal nexus at all: to harass, harm, hunt, wound, kill, trap or collect a listed animal, where harm includes habitat change that injures it. Threatened species get their prohibitions from a 4(d) rule, which for most matches the endangered ones. Plants are covered only on federal land or where state law is broken. For a buyer who intends to clear, grade, build or add near mapped habitat, this sets the order of operations: species check first, biologist second, permits third. The Groundsheet scale weights the section at 6 percent from the distance to the nearest habitat; Aptos scores High at 0.3 mi.

What you can do

  1. Before any ground disturbance, run the free federal species check for the parcel; it lists every listed species and critical habitat a project must consider. ipac.ecosphere.fws.gov
  2. If the work needs a federal permit, loan or grant, that agency must consult the wildlife service first; with no federal hook, harming a listed animal is still an offence, so have a biologist walk the site before clearing or grading. fws.gov
  3. If harm cannot be avoided, a habitat conservation plan is the private-land route to a permit; it is slow, so start early. fws.gov
  4. Read each species' own page: its status, the rule that lists it, and the habitat it needs. ecos.fws.gov

Reading this section

E · T
Endangered: in danger of extinction across all or a significant part of its range. Threatened: likely to become endangered in the foreseeable future.
Critical habitat
Ground mapped by rule as essential to a species' recovery; the polygons on the plate. Many listed species have none designated.
ESA
The Endangered Species Act of 1973, the federal law protecting species at risk of extinction.
Section 7
The consultation duty on federal agencies; reaches private land only through a federal nexus.
Section 9
The prohibition on take; applies on private land with no federal nexus.
Take
Harass, harm, hunt, wound, kill, trap or collect; harm includes habitat change that injures.
4(d) rule
Sets the prohibitions for a threatened species; the blanket rule matches endangered ones.
Federal nexus
A federal permit, funding or approval tied to a project; it brings Section 7 into play.
IPaC
Information for Planning and Consultation, the USFWS tool that returns the official species list for a project footprint.
ECOS
The Environmental Conservation Online System, the USFWS species database; its county list and its species codes.
Designated, Rule, FR
The date of the Federal Register rule that mapped the species' critical habitat, and its citation (volume and first page).
mi, bearing
Statute miles from the property point to the nearest polygon edge, and the compass direction to it.
USFWS
The US Fish and Wildlife Service, which lists species and maps critical habitat.
