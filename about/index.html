The report's own voice, and who is behind it

Groundsheet is a small company that makes one document well. It began as an engine that could draw every public map for an address on the same paper, and became a report when the maps started answering buyers' questions.

What we make

One report, for one address, from the public record. Sixteen sections, each answering one question a buyer asks about the ground under a house and the half-mile around it, each from a named public source with the date it was retrieved and the vintage of the data, each scored 0 to 3 so that the sixteen can be read together on one ladder. A codes key at the back with a line for every code, acronym and threshold the report printed, because a document that makes you look things up has not finished its job.

The voice is the report's own: plain words, the finding before the topic, every figure with its source and date, the limit stated next to the claim. No adjectives that sell. If a page cannot say something true about the ground at that address, it is left out rather than filled.

What we do not make

A Phase I environmental site assessment. A home inspection. A statutory disclosure. A measurement of radon, water, soil or air at the house. An insurance, engineering, geological or legal opinion. A climate projection. A rating of schools or neighbourhoods. The report says so on its cover and the method page says so at length, and the draft terms will say so in the words counsel chooses.

How it is made

An address is geocoded by the US Census geocoder. The engine asks 24 public services (EPA, FEMA, USGS, USFWS, USFS, EIA, NOAA, NPS, the Census, the state geological surveys and air boards) what they hold within defined radii of that point, draws each map on the same paper with the same marks, scores each section by printed rules, reconciles every figure to the record it came from, and renders the PDF and the web page. Every source, radius, weight and threshold is printed in the report. The two sample reports are public in full, every page.

Who

Groundsheet is a product of Ashcroft and Thorn, a California business. The founder writes the rules the scorer follows, reviews every sample page by page, and makes the first hundred calls to agents and inspectors himself. The entity line on this page and on the legal pages is confirmed with counsel before launch; until then it is the working name.

Groundsheet has a sibling product for Section 8 housing voucher holders, Voucher Atlas, which shares a mailbox and a philosophy and no code.

Where

Santa Cruz County, California, first; the engine renders anywhere the federal data reaches, and the samples are California and Florida because those are the states where a report has been checked page by page. Contact is email; we answer ourselves.
