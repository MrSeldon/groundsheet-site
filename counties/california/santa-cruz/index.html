Santa Cruz County sits between the Santa Cruz Mountains and Monterey Bay, which is why its public records read the way they do: a long list of coastal and sandhill species, a fire history dominated by one lightning complex, soft valley ground that amplifies earthquake shaking, and clean air most days of the year. This page walks through what each federal and state record says for the county, using one real address as the worked example: 123 Post Office Drive in Aptos, the Groundsheet sample report of 13 October 2026. Where a figure belongs to the whole county (the species list, the radon zone) it says so; where it belongs to that one point (facilities, flood zone, wells) it is a worked example, and your address will differ. Every figure below links the page of the sample report it came from.

What this page is not: a disclosure, a Phase I environmental site assessment, a home inspection or an insurance opinion. It is a reading of public records with their dates and limits stated. For an address of your own, order a groundsheet; for the method, see sources and method.

35 listed species on the county list; at the sample point, critical habitat for 11 of them within about 20 miles, the nearest 0.3 mi away

The US Fish and Wildlife Service lists 35 species under the Endangered Species Act whose range touches Santa Cruz County: 24 endangered and 11 threatened, from the southern sea otter and the marbled murrelet to the Zayante band-winged grasshopper, an insect known only from the county's sandhills. That roster is the ECOS county list and it is where a biologist starts, not a finding about any one parcel. Critical habitat is narrower: ground USFWS has mapped by rule as essential to a species' recovery. At the Aptos sample point, 11 of the 35 have critical habitat mapped within about 20 miles (13 polygons); the nearest is the tidewater goby, a small lagoon fish, 0.3 mi to the SW, then the robust spineflower at 1.4 mi NE and the monterey spineflower at 2.2 mi E. None of the polygons contains the sample point.

Limits. A county list says a species' range touches the county, not that it occurs on a street. A habitat polygon 0.3 mi away binds no private owner by itself; what the law does on private land turns on whether a project has a federal permit, loan or grant (Section 7) and, for animals, on the take prohibition that applies everywhere (Section 9). Who to call: before any ground disturbance, run the free federal species check at IPaC; if clearing or grading is planned near mapped habitat, a consulting biologist walks the site first. see this page of the sample report (page 23).

23 mapped burn perimeters since 2015 in the sample's search, 560,243 acres, led by the SCU Lightning Complex of 2020

The county's fire record since 2015 is the record of August 2020. The CZU Lightning Complex burned 85,839 acres of the Santa Cruz Mountains, its nearest edge 11.1 mi west of the sample point; the SCU Lightning Complex, the largest in the search at 405,644 acres, lies 27.1 mi to the north-east across the Santa Clara Valley; the River fire burned 51,566 acres 28.2 mi to the south-east. Of the 23 perimeters the sample found, 10 are prescribed or vegetation-management burns, which appear because they are mapped perimeters, not because they were wildfires. The nearest perimeter of any kind is 8.6 mi away. The US Forest Service's Wildfire Hazard Potential at the sample point is class 2 of 5, low: the model describes the standing landscape, the history describes what has burned.

Limits. The search radius is 50 km (31 mi) from the sample point; the sample report captions it "thirty miles" and 4 of the 23 perimeters fall between 30 and 35 miles by their nearest edge, which the report team is reconciling. Distances marked "about" are to the nearest mapped vertex, computed for this page. Fires under 100 acres and fires before 2015 are not drawn. The NIFC record is incomplete before 2021, which is why MTBS is merged in. Who to call: the county's hazard zone is a separate question, answered by the State Fire Marshal's map (see the hazard zones below); for the house itself, a defensible-space inspection from the local fire agency. see this page of the sample report (page 15).

EPA radon Zone 2 for the county: 1.8 pCi/L median in 342 tested homes, 8.2 percent at or above the action level

The EPA classes Santa Cruz County as Zone 2, the middle of three: a predicted indoor average of 2 to 4 pCi/L, under the 4 pCi/L action level and above outdoor air. Two county-wide figures sit under that class. The California Department of Public Health radon program aggregate for 2015-01 to 2019-12 gives a median of 1.8 pCi/L across 342 tested homes, with 8.2 percent at or above 4. And the USGS surface-uranium model from the NURE airborne survey gives 1.26 ppm equivalent uranium at the sample point, low, below the national median of 1.6 ppm: the ground here carries relatively little of the uranium that feeds indoor radon.

Limits. A county class and a county median cannot tell you this house's number; two houses on one street test differently. The homes in the state survey chose to test, so the median is a spread, not a rate for any street. The NURE lines were flown 5 to 10 km apart and modelled to a grid in 2020. Who to call: nobody yet; a short-term test kit in the lowest lived-in level, 2 to 7 days with the house closed, from the state radon program or a hardware store, then a certified mitigator only if a 90-day test confirms 4 pCi/L or more. see this page of the sample report (page 20).

50 wells on record within 2 mi of the sample point, 32 with a depth; median bottom 285 ft; a public-supply well 0.17 mi away

Aptos is on city water, yet the well record around the sample point is dense: 50 completion reports within 2 mi, all of them within 0.80 mi, with 29 inside a quarter mile. By recorded use: 18 unknown, 15 domestic (do), 6 other, 4 monitor (mo), 4 irrigation (ir), 2 water supply / public supply (ws), 1 industrial (in). 32 of the 50 carry a drilled depth, with a median bottom of 285 ft and a deepest of 743 ft; 13 carry a yield, median 15 gpm. The nearest well on record is 0.16 mi SW, a monitoring well (one that watches water near a tank or cleanup site); the nearest public-supply well, a drinking-water system's, is 0.17 mi NNE, 743 ft deep at 250 gpm, a receptor where anything moving through the ground from a regulated facility would show first.

Limits. A completion report records the well as drilled, not as it is today; many are unnamed as to use and most lack a yield. The database is national and lags the state's own well log system. Who to call: on a private well, a certified lab for bacteria and nitrate every year and a one-time panel for what the nearby facilities handle; the completion report itself is at the California well completion report archive. see this page of the sample report (page 18).

Seismic Design Category D at the sample point, high shaking, on alluvial fan deposits of the Pleistocene, 0.01 to 2.58 Ma

The building code's seismic design category at the sample point is D, high: strong shaking expected in the life of a building; the code requires ductile detailing, anchored equipment and engineered foundations. The mapped design values are SDS 0.96 g and SD1 0.4 g (from SS 1.59 and S1 0.6 g before the site adjustment), with a peak ground acceleration of 0.64 g at the 2 percent in 50 years motion, on an assumed firm site class BC (VS30 760 m/s). The ground under the sample is alluvial fan deposits, Pleistocene, 0.01 to 2.58 Ma: loose sand, gravel and mud dropped by streams leaving the hills: it drains well and holds shallow groundwater, but it is soft ground that amplifies earthquake shaking and settles unevenly

Limits. The design category describes the ground, not the building; the mapped values assume firm ground, and soft alluvium like this amplifies shaking, so a soils report settles what is under the footing. California's own fault and seismic hazard zones are a separate statutory question, answered in the hazard zones below. Who to call: a home inspector for the bolting, cripple-wall bracing and water-heater strap; a structural engineer for a retrofit; a geotechnical engineer where a seismic hazard zone is mapped. see this page of the sample report (page 18).

The six statutory hazard zones at the sample point: outside all six of california's statutory hazard zones; nearest edge the state responsibility area at 0.02 mi

California Civil Code 1103 makes a seller disclose whether a property lies in any of six mapped hazard zones. Read from the same public maps for the sample point, the answer is No to all six, with the nearest edges in the table; the point sits in the local responsibility area for fire protection, nearest zone edge: state responsibility area, 0.02 mi nne. The Newell dam inundation area is the nearest dam path at 6.2 mi west; the nearest earthquake fault zone is 5.5 mi east on the Watsonville West quadrangle, and no fault zone or seismic hazard zone map has been released for the sample's own quadrangle, which means no zones there, mapped or not.

Good air on 347 of 360 days reported in 2025 at the county's EPA monitor, Santa Cruz, 4.9 mi from the sample point

The EPA monitor nearest the sample point is the Santa Cruz site at 960 Bostwick Lane (060870007), 4.9 mi to the west. In 2025 it reported a certified 24-hour PM2.5 value on 360 days: 347 Good, 13 Moderate and none worse, with the worst day 15 Mar 2025. Against the three-year standards the monitor meets both PM2.5 standards: a 5.3 µg/m³ three-year mean against 9, a 11.5 µg/m³ 98th-percentile day against 35, and an ozone design value of 49 ppb against 70. The one bad stretch in three years was 19 to 24 September 2023, when smoke from the Quarry fire about 128 mi north-east pushed one day to 47.4 µg/m³ (AQI 130, unhealthy for sensitive groups).

Limits. One monitor speaks for the county's air at that monitor; a house in a canyon or beside a highway differs. EPA's attainment designations cover whole areas, not one site. Who to call: nobody; the fire and smoke map on a bad day, and a filter rated MERV 13 on the furnace. see this page of the sample report (page 32).

263 EPA-regulated facilities within 2 mi of the sample point, none of them Tier I or II

Aptos Village is a working village, and the record shows it: 10 regulated facilities within a quarter mile of the sample point, 44 within half a mile, 164 within a mile and 263 within two, nearly all of them RCRA registrations for ordinary businesses (a medical office, an equipment yard, a tyre shop, a dry cleaner, a garage). None carries a Tier I designation (Superfund, leaking tank, treatment-storage-disposal, significant non-compliance) or Tier II (large-quantity generator, TRI reporter), and no leaking underground storage tank is listed. Volume alone cannot lift this section past Moderate in the Groundsheet scale; composition is the lens.

Limits. Registration is not release: a facility on the list handles regulated material under permit, which is what the list records. Proximity is screened as a proxy for the three routes a release can travel (air, water, land); the screen flags what warrants a closer look and does not model exposure. Who to call: an environmental professional for a Phase I assessment if a Tier I or II facility were near; here, the one practical step is asking the dry cleaner's and the garage's compliance history in EPA ECHO. see this page of the sample report (page 8).

FEMA Zone X at the sample point, minimal flood hazard; the nearest special flood hazard area, Zone AE, 0.09 mi SW

The sample point is in Zone X, area of minimal flood hazard: outside the mapped 1-percent-annual-chance floodplain, so mandatory flood insurance does not apply. The ground is 104 ft above sea level (NAVD 88, USGS 3DEP). The nearest special flood hazard area is Zone AE along the creek 0.09 mi to the SW. Santa Cruz County's floodplains follow its creeks to the bay, and the county's FIRM panels (06087C) are the legally in-force maps.

Limits. About a quarter of flood claims come from outside the mapped floodplain; a Zone X lot still floods. Flood polygons are drawn at regional scale, not survey grade; a boundary 0.09 mi away is close enough to confirm, not to read off the map. Who to call: a surveyor for an elevation certificate if the lot is near an edge; an insurance agent for the low-risk policy quote before closing. see this page of the sample report (page 12).

Limits. NWI polygons are drawn from imagery at planning scale: edges can be off by tens of feet and small or forested wetlands are often missed, so the map is a reason to look, not a boundary. Since Sackett (2023) only wetlands with a continuous surface connection to a permanent water are federal; California's Regional Water Quality Control Board regulates fill beyond that, and in the coastal zone the local plan adds sensitive-habitat buffers, often 100 ft. Who to call: a wetland delineator and the Corps for a jurisdictional determination before any grading near the mapped edge. see this page of the sample report (page 27).

7 natural gas transmission segments within 5 km of the sample point, the nearest centreline 0.8 km (0.5 mi) to the west; no hazardous-liquid line

One operator, California Gas Transmission Co, runs the 7 segments the public EIA layer shows within 5 km, 3 of them within 1 km; none is interstate and there is no hazardous-liquid line. Limits. The EIA geometry is generalised, not a survey line; the precise alignment is in the national pipeline mapping system, which the public views one county at a time. Setbacks are local, not federal. Who to call: 811 two working days before any digging; the title company for any easement on the parcel. see this page of the sample report (page 29).
