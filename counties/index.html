How a county page is made

Every county page starts as a Groundsheet report for one real address in that county. The page keeps the report's figures, maps and collars (source, vintage, date retrieved, projection) and adds what a county-wide reader needs: which figures belong to the whole county (the species list, the radon zone, the surface-uranium survey) and which belong to the one sample point (facilities, flood zone, wells, pipelines), the limits of each record, the specialist each finding calls for, and a link to the page of the sample report it came from.

The rule, from the first day of planning this site: no county page without verified local figures, original explanation, a dated map, citations and limits. A thin page that only swaps a county name into a template is worse than no page, for the reader and for the search engines that now penalise it.

Coming by launch state

Groundsheet launches in California first, then Colorado, Washington, Oregon, Florida and North Carolina. County pages follow the reports: as each county gets its first run, its page is built from that run. The planned first thirty pages, in order, begin with California (Santa Cruz, then Santa Clara, Sonoma, Marin, Los Angeles and San Diego), then Jefferson, Boulder and Denver in Colorado; King and Snohomish in Washington; Deschutes in Oregon; Miami-Dade and Hillsborough in Florida; Wake in North Carolina. The Tampa sample report already covers Hillsborough County; its page is next.

If you want a county sooner, or you are an agent or inspector who works one, say so: a county page costs one report.
