How it works, in three steps

  1. 1Give us the address. We geocode it with the US Census geocoder and show you the point on a satellite image before anything runs. You confirm the house, not a guess.
  2. 2Wait a few minutes. The engine asks 24 public federal and state services about that point, draws every map, scores sixteen sections 0 to 3 each, and checks every figure against its record.
  3. 3Read it two ways. A 40-page PDF with the cover, the summary, each section and a codes key for every term, plus a web page with the same findings that you can forward to your agent or inspector.

What it is, and what it is not

Groundsheet reads 24 public federal and state services for one address and prints what they hold: the regulated facilities within two miles and their status, the flood zone, the wildfire record, the wells on file and their depths, the radon zone, the listed species and their habitat, the wetlands, the pipelines, the schools and clinics, a year of air, and in Florida the sinkholes, in California the six statutory zones. Each finding is scored 0 to 3 and the sixteen scores make one composite with a word beside it. Every map carries its source, the date it was retrieved and the vintage of the data.

It is a screening report. It is not a Phase I environmental site assessment, not a home inspection, not a California Natural Hazard Disclosure Statement, not an insurance, engineering or legal opinion, and it does not measure anything at the house. It tells you what the public record says about the ground, in plain words, so that you know which questions to ask and whom to ask. The limits are printed beside each claim and again on the sources and method page.
