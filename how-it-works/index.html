An address in, a sourced report out, in minutes

You give us one street address. We geocode it, ask 24 public services what they hold for that point, draw every map with its source and date, score sixteen sections, and hand you a PDF and a web page you can forward.

The three steps

  1. Step 1The address. Type it in. The US Census geocoder returns a point and a match quality (exact, non-exact, or none). We show the point on a satellite image and you confirm it is the house you mean. The cover prints the match quality, because a report for the wrong point is worse than none.
  2. Step 2The minutes. The engine asks 24 services, EPA, FEMA, USGS, USFWS, USFS, EIA, NOAA, NPS, the Census and the state surveys, what they hold around that point within defined radii. It draws each map on the same paper, scores each section 0 to 3, builds the composite, and reconciles every printed figure to the record it came from. Most reports finish in under ten minutes.
  3. Step 3The PDF and the web page. You get a 40-page PDF: cover, summary, the sections, how the report was made, and a codes key with a line for every code it printed. You also get a web page with the same findings at a private link, so the agent, the inspector and the lender can read it without a download.

What each section does

Each section answers one question from one or two public sources, states the finding first, draws the map with its collar (source, retrieved date, data vintage, projection, north), gives the figures, says what the source cannot tell you, and ends with what a buyer does next: the kit to buy, the test to order, the office to call, the specialist to hire. The sections pages walk through all thirteen with the real figures from the samples; the sources and method page lists every source with its vintage.

The eight questions people ask

Is this a Phase I environmental site assessment?

No. A Phase I under ASTM E1527-21 involves a site visit, interviews, a historical records review and an environmental professional's opinion, and it confers CERCLA liability protections. Groundsheet does none of that. It is a screening report that reads public databases for one point and tells you what they hold. If a Groundsheet finding is serious, a Phase I or a specialist is what comes next.

Does it replace the home inspection?

No. The inspector checks the house: roof, foundation, wiring, plumbing, the things you can see and touch. Groundsheet checks the ground: what the public record says about the land and the surroundings, from flood zones to wells to regulated facilities. Buyers use both in the same contingency week.

Does it replace the California Natural Hazard Disclosure?

No. The NHD statement under Civil Code 1103 is a seller's statutory disclosure of six mapped zones. The California insert in a Groundsheet report reads the same six public maps and shows them, but it is not the statutory form and it is not a substitute for it. Groundsheet is a buyer-side supplement.

How current is the data?

Every map carries the date it was retrieved and the vintage of the dataset behind it, because the two differ: a FEMA flood map may have been retrieved today and last revised years ago. The report is a snapshot of the public record at the time it was run. Members can rerun the same address during escrow at no charge while the data moves.

What happens if a source fails?

Public services go down. If one does not answer, that section is marked Not screened, the cover line changes from "Checks: passed" to "Checks: flagged", the method page lists the missing source, and you receive a credit for a rerun once the source is back. The score never silently fills in a missing section.

Can my agent put their name on it?

Yes, through the agent membership. The cover carries a "Presented by" line with the agent's name, licence and brokerage; the report keeps its own authorship line, "Research and report by Groundsheet", and its method stays the same. Agents buy the report for the client; Groundsheet pays no referral fees.

Can I forward it?

Yes, to the people in your transaction: your agent, your inspector, your lender, your attorney, a co-buyer, the seller. The licence is for that transaction. It is not for attaching to a listing or reselling. The draft terms set this out and counsel is reviewing them.

What does the score mean?

Each of the sixteen sections is scored 0 to 3 and shown as 0, 3.3, 6.7 or 10. The composite is the weighted sum on a 0 to 10 scale, and the word beside it, Low, Moderate, Elevated, High or Severe, is the band the composite falls in. It is Groundsheet's weighting of public records, not a government rating and not a measurement. A single High section can sit inside a Moderate composite, which is why the cover also lists the sections that warrant follow-up.
